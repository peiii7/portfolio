Project
Overview

Traditional tea houses carry deep cultural heritage, yet face two kinds of attrition: younger generations find them old-fashioned, while regulars are slowed down by overly literary menus and slow service. Using a double-diamond process starting from field research, we designed a dual-track system letting customers with different tea knowledge each take the path that fits them.

Problem

  • Tea culture lacked an easy entry point

    Its cultural depth read as "old-fashioned" to younger generations, with no relaxed starting point for learning about tea.

  • The menu's language was hard to understand

    Product names and descriptions were overly literary, making it hard for less knowledgeable customers to decide what to order.

  • Service efficiency suffered at peak hours

    Ordering relied on one-on-one explanations from staff, leading to long waits that traded off against the experience.

Goal

  • Put knowledge inside the experience

    Replace one-way explanations with a gamified gathering flow so customers learn tea's flavor and effects through exploration.

  • Rewrite the menu in the customer's language

    Use intuitive categories and concrete descriptions so even beginners can decide on their own.

  • Run two tracks in parallel without sacrificing efficiency

    Give regulars and newcomers separate flows so the added game never slows down service.

How might a dual-track system redefine the tea house service experience?

Outcome 01

Visiting traditional tea houses to find real breakpoints via service blueprint

We experienced the full service flow at tea houses like Wistaria Tea House and mapped a four-layer service blueprint and empathy maps. The breakpoints converged on one thing: customers couldn't read the menu, so they pushed decisions back onto staff—which is exactly how peak-hour waits accumulated.

Outcome 02

Two personas leading to a "dual track," not a single best answer

A 54-year-old business owner familiar with tea culture was blocked by overly complex naming and set combinations; a 28-year-old teacher seeking quiet relaxation was blocked by having almost no tea knowledge. Their obstacles differed—forcing both into one flow would satisfy neither.

Outcome 03

Designing a dual-track ordering system for regulars and newcomers

Gamified ordering lets customers play an adventurer, gathering ingredients to compose their own tea; standard ordering keeps an intuitive flow supporting LINE Pay and cash. Both paths converge into one fulfillment logic, so the backend never has to maintain two systems.

Outcome 04

Defining a 3D art style, turning six tea types into six scenes

As lead artist, I chose a Low Poly / Cartoon style that stayed recognizable within a tight timeline while keeping modeling cost feasible. The six exploration scenes were designed around the real growing regions of black, green, dark, yellow, oolong, and white tea.

Take away

Gamification isn't an add-on—it's redesigning the service's skeleton

The effective approach was returning to the service blueprint and letting game mechanics take over the explanation work staff used to do—solving an efficiency problem, not just adding fun.

Art style must serve both the brand and production reality

As lead artist, I learned style decisions can't be based on aesthetics alone. Low Poly was the result of weighing recognizability against modeling hours available.
