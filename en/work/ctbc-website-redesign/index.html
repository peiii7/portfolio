Project
Overview

CTBC's personal banking website spans 23 product departments, with content accumulated over years in inconsistent styles. The client had only a narrow homepage guideline, with no extensible design system. The goal was a website that felt new, was easy to navigate, could scale, and met WCAG AA sitewide. Among 3 UI designers, I was responsible for the visual direction, key pages like AI search, and building the design system.

Problem

  • A steady but middle-aged brand image struggled to reach younger customers

    Brand research showed CTBC as stable but undifferentiated, with market-leading services like fraud protection buried too deep.

  • Complex information architecture made navigation hard

    Content was categorized by product and department rather than user context; on-site search underperformed, requiring repeated back-and-forth clicks.

  • Technical and organizational constraints led to inconsistent experiences

    Different departments authored pages independently via AEM with no unified rules for minisite links, and no design system to follow.

Goal

  • Shape a young, smart, distinctive brand image

    Break from typical financial-industry design and echo current tech aesthetics to shift the "steady but middle-aged" impression.

  • Improve findability and move the user journey forward

    Reorganize content by usage context and strengthen interaction guidance to drive traffic, satisfaction, and applications.

  • Establish design rules that can scale without drifting

    Accommodate many products and services with room for future growth, meeting accessibility guidelines sitewide.

When users already find answers off-site, how does the website become new, findable, and scalable again?

Outcome 01

Turning abstract brand aspirations into an executable visual vocabulary

The client wanted "modern, intelligent, era-defining" while shifting away from "steady but middle-aged." I broke "intelligent" into three designable qualities: color flow, visual depth, and dynamic motion. I proposed two mutually exclusive directions—A) "Life as it is," user-centered with rounded, soft layers; B) "Precisely Yours," brand-centered with dark tones and geometric rhythm—and converged with the client over 5+ rounds.

Outcome 02

Repositioning the website's role in the journey by designing AI search

Research showed users rarely start from the homepage, instead arriving from search engines, AI tools, or social media to verify details. I designed an overlay-style sitewide search with a consistent star icon marking AI content, prioritizing CTAs over raw information while preserving links back to source pages.

Outcome 03

Building a design system from 0 to 1 for 23 departments to share

Starting from color and type tokens, I built up component types, scenarios, and usage rules so the same modules could recombine into different product pages. Priorities came from a workshop where 52 members in 7 groups sorted features by urgency vs. maintenance cost. Accessibility was a default, not a checklist—AA contrast, alt text, form labels, and keyboard operation were written into base component rules.

Outcome 04

Usability testing and iterating on visual design from feedback

16 participants tested old vs. new across six scenarios. Desktop SUS rose from 62 to 84, but mobile dropped from 82 to 70—mainly because a floating bottom nav bar went unnoticed by over half the participants. I moved the nav back to an expected position, separated shortcut blocks from marketing placements, and unified the search entry point and AI labeling.

Take away

Visual discussions need a judgment standard everyone agrees on

The visual direction went through 5+ rounds. Judging by brand positioning, maintenance cost, and usage context—not personal taste—brought the conversation back to the actual problem.

Accessibility is a default for every component, not a feature of one section

Requiring WCAG AA sitewide on this project confirmed that accessibility should be a design default, not something checked after the fact.
